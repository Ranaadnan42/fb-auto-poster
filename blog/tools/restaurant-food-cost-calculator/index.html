How to calculate food cost for a menu item

Food cost is the ingredient cost of one portion, divided by its menu price. The formula is simple, but getting the ingredient costs right is where most owners make mistakes.

  1. Cost each ingredient per unit: pack price divided by pack size. A 1,000 g pack of chicken at $9 costs $0.009 per gram.
  2. Adjust for yield: trimming, bones, peel and cooking loss mean you use more than you serve. If only 90% of the chicken is usable, divide by 0.9.
  3. Multiply by the amount used in the recipe and add up all ingredients, including sauces, oil and garnish.
  4. Divide by portions if the recipe makes more than one serving.
  5. Price the dish: menu price = portion cost / target food cost %. At a 30% target, a $2.40 portion should sell for about $8.00.

What is a good food cost percentage?

Most restaurants aim for 25-35% overall. Coffee and drinks often run 10-20%, while steak or seafood dishes can sit at 35-40% because the cash profit per plate is still high. Look at both the percentage and the gross profit in dollars before changing a price.

Ways to lower food cost without raising prices

  • Weigh portions for your most-sold dishes and train staff on them.
  • Re-price recipes whenever supplier prices change, not once a year.
  • Use trim from one dish in another (soups, sauces, staff meals).
  • Track waste daily; a POS with inventory tracking makes this much easier.

Frequently asked questions

What is the formula for food cost percentage?

Food cost % = (ingredient cost of one portion / menu price) x 100. For example, a dish that costs $3 to make and sells for $10 has a 30% food cost.

How do I set a menu price from food cost?

Divide the portion cost by your target food cost as a decimal. A $2.50 portion at a 30% target gives $2.50 / 0.30 = about $8.35, which you can round to a menu-friendly price.

Does food cost include labour and packaging?

No. Food cost is ingredients only. Packaging is often tracked separately, and labour plus food together are called prime cost, which many restaurants keep under about 60-65% of sales.

Why should I include yield percentage?

Because you rarely serve 100% of what you buy. Trimming, peeling and cooking loss raise the true cost per usable gram, and ignoring it makes dishes look more profitable than they are.
